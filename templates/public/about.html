About the Program

Background & Origin

This program builds on a first attempt. In December 2023, six students from Shamva spent three weeks during their school holiday learning the basics of C, C++, and electronics — taught with almost no resources: one borrowed laptop, two sets of borrowed electronics components, and mobile phones as coding devices. The program confirmed what earlier research had already shown: students in Shamva are deeply curious and capable, but they have had almost no exposure to computer science, programming, or modern technology. Of the 51 students surveyed at Wadzanai High School beforehand, not a single one knew any programming language.

This second program goes further. Rather than teaching general programming, it focuses on the foundations of AI engineering — one of the most important and fast-growing fields in the world — in a way that is accessible, practical, and directly tied to the problems students see in their own lives: drug abuse, water and electricity shortages, early child marriage, and high unemployment. The goal isn't just exposure to technology. It's building the foundation for an independent, self-sustaining organization that equips young people in Shamva with the skills and mindset to build solutions to their community's problems.

The format reflects the lessons learned the first time around. Students meet twice a week for live Zoom lessons taught remotely, plus a weekly hands-on lab session, all from a dedicated room at Wadzanai High School. Six weeks take students from their first lines of Python code to a fully built, deployed AI-powered web application — one that they host, share, and can point to and say: I made that, and you can use it.

Why This Program Matters

Young people in Shamva grow up in a community where the most visible economic path is small-scale gold mining. Schools do not teach robotics, and while computer science is technically part of the curriculum, it rarely emphasizes computational thinking, problem solving, or real-world application. Internet access is limited, and exposure to fields like AI — fields that are rapidly reshaping the global economy — is almost nonexistent.

This matters for two reasons. First, AI and software skills open up career paths that most students in Shamva have never had reason to consider. Second, and just as importantly, these skills are tools for something bigger: a student who understands how AI works can start to think about how to use it to address drug abuse in their neighborhood, improve access to medicine, or help girls stay in school. The technical knowledge is the means. Community problem-solving is the purpose.

How It Works

school

Learn

Six weeks of live, mentor-led lessons covering Python, Streamlit, and AI APIs — two Zoom sessions a week, plus a hands-on lab session where students build what they've just learned.

build

Build

Students turn each week's lessons into real software, working on problems they've identified in their own community — from drug-abuse awareness to early warning systems and access to information.

rocket_launch

Deploy

In the final week, every student hosts their project live on the internet and presents it at a community showcase — a real, working application that anyone with the link can open and use.

Six Weeks, Start to Finish

Weeks 1–2

Python: Learning to Speak to a Computer

Students learn the core building blocks of programming — variables, conditions, loops, functions — and develop the habit of thinking like a programmer: breaking a problem into steps and translating those steps into code.

Week 3

Streamlit: Turning Code Into Something People Can Use

Students learn to wrap their Python code in a simple, interactive web interface — the moment they realize that what they're building can actually be used by other people.

Weeks 4–5

AI and APIs: Making Software That Thinks

The heart of the course. Students learn how AI models work conceptually and how to connect them to their own applications through APIs — building software that uses AI to do something genuinely useful.

Week 6

Final Projects: Build, Host, and Share

No new lessons — just building. Students polish their final projects, deploy them live on the internet, and present them at a community showcase.

Looking Ahead

After this cohort finishes, the plan is to collect detailed feedback from every student, revise the curriculum based on what worked and what didn't, and restart the program roughly a month later with a new cohort — each one helping make the next better. The long-term goal is a permanent club at Wadzanai High School, where graduates mentor newer students, and an independent organization that doesn't depend on any one person to keep running.
